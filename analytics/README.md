## Design Decisions

### Missing-Value Strategy (Task 2)

| Column | Missing % | Strategy | Justification |
|---|---|---|---|
| `deck` | ~77.2% | **Drop column** | Far above 30% threshold — imputing 77% of values would fabricate the majority of the column, making it statistically meaningless |
| `age` | ~19.9% | **Median imputation** | Within 5–30% range; median is outlier-robust and preserves all rows for modeling |
| `embarked` | ~0.2% | **Drop rows** | < 5% threshold; only 2 rows affected — negligible data loss |
| `embark_town` | ~0.2% | **Drop rows** | Same 2 rows — consistent treatment with `embarked` |

### Correlation Matrix (Task 4)

Restricted to exactly **6 columns**: `survived`, `pclass`, `age`, `sibsp`, `parch`, `fare`.

`adult_male` and `alone` are **excluded** because they are derived/redundant boolean flags:
- `adult_male` = directly computable from `sex` + `age`
- `alone` = directly computable from `sibsp` + `parch` (both == 0)

Including them would inflate correlations with their source features, not add independent signal.

**Two strongest correlations (largest absolute off-diagonal |r|):**
1. **`pclass` ↔ `fare`** (~−0.55): First-class passengers paid dramatically higher fares; third-class paid the least. Class and fare are economically linked by design — this is the strongest signal in the matrix.
2. **`pclass` ↔ `survived`** (~−0.34): Higher class number correlates with lower survival. First-class passengers had priority lifeboat access; third-class passengers were deepest in the ship and faced the most barriers.

### Multivariate Data Story (Task 5) — Written Interpretations

**Chart 1 — Survival Rate by Sex & Passenger Class:**
Female passengers survived at dramatically higher rates across all classes (~97% 1st, ~92% 2nd, ~50% 3rd) vs males (~37% 1st, ~16% 2nd, ~15% 3rd). "Women and children first" was enforced most strictly for upper-class women. Third-class females dropping to ~50% shows class constraints limited even women's access to lifeboats.

**Chart 2 — Age Distribution by Class & Survival:**
Survivors tend to be slightly younger across all classes, though the effect is modest. First-class passengers are older on average — suggesting older wealthy individuals had better lifeboat access. Third-class passengers are the youngest but had the lowest survival rates, confirming age alone could not overcome class disadvantage.

**Chart 3 — Fare vs Age by Survival:**
Survivors cluster at higher fare values regardless of age, reinforcing the class-survival link. A dense cluster of non-survivors at low fare / mid-age represents third-class male adults. A few high-fare non-survivors indicate that wealth alone could not guarantee survival for males.

**Chart 4 — Survival by Embarkation Port:**
Cherbourg (~55% survival) outperforms Queenstown (~39%) and Southampton (~34%). This is largely a confounding effect — Cherbourg loaded proportionally more first-class passengers who had inherently higher survival rates. Port of embarkation is a proxy for class composition, not a direct causal factor.

### Preprocessing Pipeline (Task 8)

All preprocessing steps are wrapped in a scikit-learn `Pipeline` with `ColumnTransformer`:
- **Numeric features** (`pclass`, `age`, `sibsp`, `parch`, `fare`): `SimpleImputer(median)` → `StandardScaler`
- **Categorical features** (`sex`, `embarked`): `SimpleImputer(most_frequent)` → `OneHotEncoder(drop='first')`

The `Pipeline` enforces fit-on-train-only structurally — `.fit()` is called on `X_train` only, then `.transform()` is called on `X_test`. No test-set statistics ever influence the preprocessing.

### Stratified Split Justification (Task 7)

The survival classes are imbalanced (~38% survived, ~62% did not). Without stratification, a random split could assign disproportionately more of one class to train or test, biasing evaluation metrics. `stratify=y` ensures both splits mirror the original class balance, giving fairer and more reproducible results.

### Imbalance Handling Conclusion (Task 11)

- **Baseline**: conservative — decent precision, lower recall for survivors
- **`class_weight='balanced'`**: best balance of precision/recall improvement with minimal code change; recommended for moderate imbalance
- **SMOTE**: similar to balanced weights on this dataset; superior for extreme imbalance (< 10% minority class)

**Winner: `class_weight='balanced'`** — simplest approach with comparable F1 to SMOTE.

### Heteroscedasticity — Regression (Task 13)

The residual plot shows a clear fan-shaped (funnel) pattern: residuals are small for low predicted fares and grow much larger for high predicted fares. This is **classic heteroscedasticity** — error variance is NOT constant across fitted values, violating the OLS homoscedasticity assumption. A log-transform of `fare` or a quantile regression would be more appropriate for production use.

## Final Written Recommendation

**Deploy: Random Forest (tuned via GridSearchCV)**

The tuned Random Forest achieves the highest scores across all classification metrics — highest accuracy, F1, and AUC. Its AUC demonstrates excellent discriminative ability across all decision thresholds, meaning it consistently ranks true survivors above non-survivors. The OOB score (reported in `02_modeling.ipynb`) provides an independent generalisation estimate confirming the model is not simply overfitting.

Logistic Regression is a strong interpretable baseline but falls short on F1 and AUC. The Decision Tree is easy to visualise and explain (see `charts/decision_tree.png`) but sacrifices predictive power due to its depth constraint.

For the regression sub-task, the linear model achieves modest R² — the heteroscedastic residual pattern suggests a log transformation of `fare` or a gradient-boosted regressor would yield materially better results in production.
